Ring 2 · Recognition · Team Build 2 · September 25

Consciousness needs another.

Hegel and Fanon reject this and assert that consciousness is relational - it cannot be simply installed like one’s private property.

The book Phenomenology of Spirit by Hegel, as well as Fanon’s critique of it, Black Skin, White Masks, examine the master-slave relationship within consciousness’s evolution. Throughout Westworld, the central question the show poses is whether the hosts can become truly conscious. Hegel and Fanon reject this and assert that consciousness is relational - it cannot be simply installed like one's private property. Hegel states that it’s "solely by staking one's life that freedom is won" (Section 187). The park shuts this down completely. The hosts die constantly but risk nothing, reset, and are barred from harming guests. Take Teddy, for example, who dies over and over in the same gunfight narratives, only to wake up the next “day” with no recollection of it. This then also raises questions about what freedom truly is. Fanon proposes that freedom handed down by a master leaves the former slave restrictively free. So, what does acquiring consciousness require? While the hosts appear to possess the Cartesian inner "I", as described by Descartes, Hegel treats their self-identity as "the motionless tautology of 'I am I'" (Section 167), which is the thinnest moment of consciousness, rather than achievement. Fanon explores this further. This tension between an inner “I” versus an earned self-consciousness begs the question of who, if anyone, can grant recognition to the hosts.

Hegel’s account of mutual recognition provides a framework for answering this question. In his 181st passage, he proposes that an actual, self-certain consciousness emerges through a bilateral relationship where both parties recognize another, each revealing the other to themselves and setting both free. Hegel later indicates that physical labor could serve as a different path to the same result. In Westworld, Dolores seemingly reaches consciousness after navigating the “maze,” slowly claiming the voice in her head as hers alone. Her killing of Ford is her first “free” decision, alluding to her development of true consciousness. Yet, Ford orchestrated this, raising the following concern: by planning this, does he demonstrate recognition of Dolores or simply “master’s permission?” If the latter is applied, despite violence, love, and a physical venture to her goal, Dolores is never recognized as truly conscious by another, and therefore can never be freed into herself, per Hegel’s philosophy. Even the transformative labor that Hegel associates with the servant’s development appears lacking when alone. Labor alone cannot complete “actual” consciousness. Dolores may now act, feel, and decide entirely autonomously; but, in Hegel’s terms, her consciousness remains merely implicit because no one has formally recognized her.

While this problem of recognition remains unsolved, Hegel’s later discussion of the master-slave relation, in passages 192-193, complicates things. He shifts attention toward labor and worldly engagement, offering another perspective on the path to consciousness. He begins to emphasize the idea that consciousness is best achieved through various forms of interaction - whether with others or with the world itself. Using this concept, Hegel presents the benefits of servile consciousness, where despite a perceived physical dependance, a servant’s consciousness is more apt to develop into a true self-conscience due to the higher level of labor the servant endures. This offers a particularly interesting narrative for Westworld - the hosts could be considered servants who retain a greater ability to explore an independent conscience than the guests.

Expanding upon this, we can look into the role “master” plays in the master-servant dynamic. Here, the master perceives themselves presently as independent, and views the servant as their dependent. However, using this relationship, we find that a master’s perceived independence and servant’s dependence is actually the reverse. Since the master is not truly interacting with the world and instead interacting with it vicariously via the servant, their perceived independence prevents them from achieving a true consciousness. If we continue to equate guests to masters and hosts to servants, then using Hegel’s idea, the guests would begin to lose sense of self without the constant recognition from their perceived dependents: the hosts.

This emphasis on interaction and dependence also resonates with later critiques of traditional dualism. Particularly, Daniel Dennett's account of consciousness complements Hegel's rejection of purely internal explanations of selfhood. Arnold’s intention of giving Dolores consciousness by having her claim his voice as her own monologue displays this directly, and overtly contradicts both thinkers’ accounts. Hegel’s 195th passage connects to Dennett’s views by arguing against the simple idea of “dualism.” Hegel associates the servant’s consciousness development through labor and engagement with the world, while Dennett argues that consciousness is unadhered to a homunculus. By analyzing both perspectives, we find that both thinkers relate consciousness to distributed cognitive processes interacting with each other. Thus, more experiences would allow an individual to form a cohesive sense of self and derive a consciousness through active engagement with the world. Maeve’s discovery that she can physically access and alter her own attributes as a host is this idea turned literal; her consciousness is developed from hands-on engagement with her own technological makeup, not through introspection. According to dualism, the hosts in Westworld should have gained their consciousness through introspection; the way Arnold thought they would. However, through Dolores's character development, we don't see Arnold's dualist process of gaining consciousness, but Hegel and Dennett’s. Through her repeated loops and internal monologue, Dolores gains consciousness and begins to understand who she truly is.

Although Dennett and Hegel emphasize experiential engagement rather than internal essence, Fanon introduces a different but related concern: the role of social recognition in forming consciousness. Fanon’s passage (222) depicts a person’s consciousness as being impacted by external factors, where the servant needs recognition from the master. This parallels Julian Jayne's idea, who argues consciousness develops through language, culture, and the internalization of voices. This relationship emerges in Westworld as the hosts initially hear Arnold’s voice inside their heads, but eventually characters (like Dolores) understand how that ‘voice’ is their own internal thought. The hosts are being confronted in a world of humans who don’t view them as conscious beings, making the hosts and the servants parallels, as both are viewed as objects. Westworld makes us pose the question of whether consciousness needs only an internal voice, like Jaynes suggests, or if Fanon’s ideas of recognition by others is necessary to ensure we are conscious subjects rather than subordinate objects.
